# TPM · Agile & Ways of Working

Agile leadership, planning, estimation, team alignment, and continuous improvement.

> **Role track:** Technical Program Manager (TPM) — these questions assess programme leadership, technical credibility, and cross-team execution. They are intentionally separate from the engineering interview question bank.

<summary><strong>TPM-A021 · You've been asked to set up a new programme from scratch multiple teams, unclear scope, aggressive timeline. Walk me through how you would structure it end-to-end.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. What would you do in the first two weeks to establish the programme's foundation?
2. How would you define governance, reporting cadence, and decision-making structures?
3. How would you align the teams on a shared delivery framework without being overly prescriptive?

**Follow-up**

Two months in, you discover that the governance structure you set up is too heavy teams are spending more time in meetings than building. How do you course-correct?

**What this tests**

Tests the TPM's ability to build programme structure from nothing one of the most challenging and most valuable TPM skills. The follow-up tests adaptability.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they describe a structured but time-boxed discovery and setup phase? Do they define governance proportional to programme complexity? Do they balance structure with team autonomy? Do they propose an Agile framework which would best suit the programme? TPM signal: Do they start with stakeholder alignment and scope clarity before building plans? TPM signal: Do they ask questions about the nature of work team needs to accomplish? TPM signal: Do they create a RACI or similar responsibility framework? TPM signal: Do they design governance to be lightweight and adjustable, not permanent and heavy? Red flag: Jumps to building a Gantt chart without understanding scope or stakeholders. Red flag: Copies a framework from their last job without adapting to context.

**Example strong answer**

"Setting up a programme from scratch is about establishing just enough structure to create momentum without over-engineering the process. Here's my approach: First two weeks foundation: Week 1: Understand and align. Stakeholder mapping: who are the decision-makers, who are the influencers, who needs to be informed? Map them on a RACI. Scope clarity: what's the programme trying to achieve? What does success look like? I'd hold a kick-off workshop with key stakeholders to align on outcomes, not features. 'What must be true six months from now?' Team landscape: who are the teams, what's their current workload, what skills do they have? Meet each team lead 1:1. Constraint identification: what's fixed (deadline, budget, team size) and what's flexible (scope, phasing, approach)? _Week 2: Plan and communicate._ Draft programme roadmap: high-level milestones, key dependencies, and decision points. Not a detailed plan a direction with checkpoints. Risk register: initial risk assessment based on what I've learned. External dependencies, team capacity, scope uncertainty. Communication plan: who gets what information, how often, in what format. First programme-wide stand-up or sync meeting to introduce the plan, get buy-in, and hear concerns. _Governance and reporting:_ I'd design governance based on programme complexity and team maturity, not a template from my last job. For a programme with 3-5 teams, I'd start with: Weekly programme sync (30 min): all team leads. Cross-team dependencies, blockers, risk updates. Not status status can be written. Bi-weekly steering committee (30 min): key stakeholders. Progress against milestones, risks, decisions needed. Monthly business review (1 hour): executive sponsor. Business outcomes, strategic direction, resource asks. Decision-making: clear escalation path. Team leads make team decisions. Programme-level decisions go to me and the product owner. Business decisions escalate to the steering committee. Nobody waits for a meeting to make an urgent decision. _Aligning on a delivery framework:_ I wouldn't impose Scrum, SAFe, or any specific framework. I'd agree on principles: regular delivery cadence, visibility into progress, retrospectives for improvement, and cross-team integration checkpoints. If teams are already running their own agile processes, I wouldn't change those. I'd add a thin programme layer on top integration cadence, shared milestones, cross-team dependencies. I'd explicitly say to the teams: 'How you work within your team is your choice. How we coordinate across teams is our shared responsibility.' _For the follow-up if governance is too heavy two months in, I'd cut ruthlessly:_ Audit every recurring meeting: what decisions does it drive? If none, cancel it or reduce frequency. Merge overlapping meetings. Often the programme sync and the technical sync cover the same ground. Shift status reporting from meetings to written async updates. Free up meeting time for decisions and problem-solving only. Ask the teams: 'Which meetings could we drop without losing anything important?' They'll tell you honestly."

<summary><strong>TPM-A022 · If you had to sacrifice one Scrum ceremony entirely from your programme's way of working, which one would you drop and why?</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. Walk me through your reasoning for which ceremony you'd remove and what value you'd be giving up.
2. How would you compensate for the loss of that ceremony - what alternative practice or mechanism would you introduce?
3. How would you make this decision collaboratively with your teams rather than imposing it top- down?

**Follow-up**

The teams push back and say they want to keep all ceremonies. But delivery velocity is suffering because of meeting overhead. How do you find the right balance?

**What this tests**

Tests critical thinking about agile practices whether the TPM understands the purpose of each ceremony or just follows the textbook. The follow-up tests facilitation skills.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they understand the purpose of each ceremony (planning = commitment, standup = coordination, review = feedback, retro = improvement)? Can they articulate what value each ceremony provides and what's lost without it? Do they propose a reasonable alternative that captures the lost value? TPM signal: Do they think about ceremony purpose rather than ceremony format? TPM signal: Do they involve teams in the decision rather than dictating it? TPM signal: Do they address the follow-up by optimising ceremonies rather than simply keeping or dropping them? Red flag: Drops retrospectives suggests no commitment to continuous improvement. Red flag: "I'd drop all ceremonies" suggests anti-process bias. Red flag: Cannot articulate the purpose of the ceremonies. Red flag: Names any one ceremony without proper reasoning.

**Example strong answer**

"If I had to drop one ceremony entirely, I'd drop the sprint review (demo). Here's my reasoning: _What sprint review provides: stakeholder feedback on completed work, visibility into progress, alignment between what was built and what was expected. These are all valuable._ _Why I'd sacrifice it: the value of sprint review can be largely captured through other_ mechanisms stakeholder demos can happen ad-hoc when features are ready rather than _waiting for a ceremony, and progress visibility can come from dashboards and written updates. The other three ceremonies are harder to replace:_ Sprint planning creates the team's commitment and focus for the sprint. Without it, you lose alignment on priorities. Daily standup provides rapid coordination on blockers. Without it, issues fester for days. Retrospective drives continuous improvement. Without it, problems repeat. This is the one I'd never sacrifice. _How I'd compensate:_ Replace the formal sprint review with lightweight stakeholder demos as features complete not at the end of the sprint but when they're done. This actually provides faster feedback. Maintain a programme dashboard showing sprint progress, completed stories, and upcoming work. Stakeholders who want visibility can check it anytime. For major milestones, keep larger demos just don't make them a bi-weekly obligation for every sprint. _Making this collaborative: I'd present the problem to the teams 'We need to reduce_ meeting overhead by 2 hours per sprint. Here's my thinking on which ceremony we could drop and how to compensate. What do you think?' I'd give them a week to try it and then assess together. If they have a better idea for which ceremony to modify, I'm open to that. _For the follow-up: if teams want to keep all ceremonies, the issue isn't the number of_ ceremonies it's how they're run. I'd work with each team to optimise: Standups: 10 minutes max, standing up, focused on 'what's blocked' not 'what I did yesterday.' Sprint planning: 1 hour max for a 2-week sprint. If it takes 3 hours, the backlog isn't refined enough. Review: 30 minutes max. Show working software, get feedback, move on. Retro: 45 minutes max. Focus on one improvement action, not a laundry list. Total: under 3 hours per sprint. If it's significantly more than that, the ceremonies are bloated and need streamlining."

<summary><strong>TPM-A023 · Tell me about a time you fundamentally disagreed with a decision your manager or programme sponsor made about how the programme should be run.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. How did you express your disagreement, and what approach did you take to present your alternative view?
2. What was the outcome - did they change their mind, did you change yours, or did you disagree and commit?
3. How did the experience affect your working relationship going forward?

**Follow-up**

If the decision turns out to be wrong months later just as you predicted how do you handle that without saying "I told you so"?

**What this tests**

Tests professional maturity and the ability to manage up. The follow-up tests emotional intelligence.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they describe a specific, real disagreement with substance? Do they present their case professionally with evidence, not emotion? Do they show the ability to disagree and commit when appropriate? Did they back their claims with data? TPM signal: Do they approach the disagreement as a difference in perspective, not a personal conflict? TPM signal: Do they maintain the relationship regardless of the outcome? TPM signal: Do they handle the follow-up with class focusing on fixing the situation, not being right? Red flag: Has never disagreed with management suggests lack of conviction. Red flag: Went over their manager's head without first trying to resolve it directly.

**Example strong answer**

"My programme sponsor wanted to consolidate three separate programme teams into one large team of 25 engineers working in a single sprint cadence. The rationale was efficiency _- 'one team, one plan, one set of meetings.' I disagreed because I'd seen this pattern_ before: a 25-person team doesn't function as a team; it functions as a crowd. Coordination _overhead would eat the efficiency gains._ How I presented my case: I didn't just say 'I disagree.' I built a one-page analysis: 'Here's what happens with 25 engineers in one sprint. Planning takes 4 hours instead of 1. Standup takes 25 minutes instead of 10. Dependencies are hidden because everything looks internal. Communication paths increase from 15 to 300.' I proposed an alternative: keep the three teams with clear ownership boundaries, but add a programme-level coordination layer a weekly sync across team leads, shared integration milestones, and a common backlog prioritisation meeting. This preserves the team autonomy that drives velocity while adding the cross-team alignment that was the sponsor's actual goal. Outcome: the sponsor heard me out but decided to try the consolidated team approach anyway. Their reasoning was that the teams had been too siloed and weren't collaborating enough, and they wanted a forcing function. I disagreed but committed. I said: 'I'll make this work to the best of my ability. Can we agree to evaluate after 4 sprints and be willing to adjust based on what we see?' Four sprints later, the data spoke for itself. Velocity had dropped 35%. Sprint planning was taking half a day. Engineers were frustrated. The sponsor acknowledged it wasn't working, and we restructured into the three-team model I'd originally proposed, with the coordination layer. What it taught me: Present your case once, clearly, with data. If you're overruled, commit and execute. Ask for a review point. 'Let's try it and evaluate at X' is much more productive than 'I told you so at X.' Sometimes people need to experience the problem to understand it. My analysis was abstractly convincing, but the lived experience of a 25-person standup was viscerally convincing. For the follow-up: when the decision proved wrong, I didn't say 'I told you so.' I said: 'The data from the last 4 sprints shows us what's working and what isn't. Here's my recommendation for restructuring.' I focused on the path forward, not the decision that got us here. The sponsor appreciated that I'd executed faithfully despite disagreeing, and that I was solution-oriented rather than vindicated. It actually strengthened the relationship because they knew they could trust me to be honest upfront and committed afterwards."

<summary><strong>TPM-A024 · Your programme is under pressure to deliver faster. Teams are already cutting corners reducing test coverage, skipping code reviews, deferring documentation. Defects are rising.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. How do you diagnose whether this is a capacity problem, a process problem, or a prioritisation problem?
2. What specific actions would you take to restore quality without significantly slowing down delivery?
3. How do you have the conversation with leadership about the trade-off between speed and sustainable quality?

**Follow-up**

Leadership says "we'll fix quality after the launch." Based on your experience, how do you respond to that?

**What this tests**

Tests pragmatic quality management and the ability to push back on unrealistic expectations with data.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they diagnose the root cause before prescribing a solution? Do they propose practical quality improvements that don't require slowing down significantly? Do they communicate the quality-speed trade-off in business terms? TPM signal: Do they use data defect rates, rework costs, deployment failure rates to make the case? TPM signal: Do they know the difference between "move fast" and "move recklessly"? TPM signal: Do they push back on "fix it later" with evidence from experience? Red flag: Accepts quality degradation as inevitable under time pressure. Red flag: No data or metrics just opinion.

**Example strong answer**

"Rising defects are a symptom, not a diagnosis. I'd start by understanding the root cause before prescribing the fix. Diagnosis approach: Capacity problem: are teams just overwhelmed? Check utilisation rates, sprint velocity trends, overtime hours. If the teams are at 120% capacity, no process change will help _- they need either more people or less scope. Process problem: are quality practices broken or missing? Check: are code reviews_ happening? What's the test coverage trend? Are defects being caught in testing or escaping to production? If defects are being introduced at the same rate but catching mechanisms are disabled, that's a process problem. _Prioritisation problem: is the scope realistic for the timeline? Are we trying to deliver 12_ months of work in 6 months? If yes, the answer is scope reduction, not faster coding. I'd interview the engineering leads and check the data. Usually it's a combination of all three, with one being dominant. Restoring quality without killing speed: Non-negotiable quality gates: code review and automated tests run on every PR, period. These catch the cheapest-to-fix defects. Cutting them saves 2 hours now and costs 20 hours in production debugging later. I'd reinstate these immediately. Test-on-change policy: don't aim for 100% coverage retroactively. But any code changed in this sprint must have tests. This builds coverage where it matters without a separate testing initiative. Defect triage: not all bugs are equal. P1 bugs get fixed immediately. P2 bugs go into the next sprint. P3 bugs go to backlog. This prevents the team from drowning in bug fixes while features pile up. Root cause analysis on production defects: spend 15 minutes after each production bug asking 'why did this escape to production?' The answers will reveal whether it's a testing gap, a review gap, or a requirements gap. The leadership conversation: I'd present the data: 'Our defect rate has tripled in 4 sprints. Each production defect costs the team an average of 8 hours to diagnose and fix. We're spending 40 hours per sprint on defects that shouldn't have reached production. That's the equivalent of losing one full-time engineer to rework.' Then the trade-off: 'Investing 20% of sprint capacity in quality practices (reviews, tests, refactoring) will reduce rework by 60%, giving us a net velocity increase within 3 sprints.' For the 'fix it later' follow-up: I'd say, with respect, that in my experience, 'later' never comes. After launch, there's always the next feature, the next deadline, the next priority. Meanwhile, the quality debt compounds. I'd ask: 'Can we agree on a specific date and dedicated capacity for the quality fix-up? If we can't commit to that now, the honest answer is that we're choosing to live with the current quality level permanently.' Making the implicit trade-off explicit usually changes the conversation."

<summary><strong>TPM-A025 · Over the past three sprints, your programme's actual delivery has been consistently 40% below what was estimated. Stakeholders are losing confidence in your timelines.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. How would you diagnose the root cause of persistent overestimation?
2. What techniques would you introduce to improve estimation accuracy across teams?
3. How would you rebuild stakeholder confidence in your programme's commitments?

**Follow-up**

One team estimates well but another is consistently off. The underperforming team's tech lead insists the work is just harder to estimate. How do you address this?

**What this tests**

Tests the TPM's approach to one of the most persistent programme management challenges. The follow-up tests whether they can distinguish between legitimate complexity and poor estimation practices.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they diagnose root causes: hidden work, underestimation of complexity, unplanned interruptions, scope creep within stories? Do they propose concrete estimation techniques: reference class, decomposition, historical velocity, confidence ranges? Do they address stakeholder trust as a separate problem requiring its own solution? TPM signal: Do they look at the data what's actually happening in sprints vs. what was planned? TPM signal: Do they use velocity as an input to future estimates rather than ignoring past performance? TPM signal: Do they handle the follow-up by validating the claim with data rather than accepting or dismissing it? Red flag: Blames engineers for bad estimates without investigating causes. Red flag: Solution is "just estimate better."

**Example strong answer**

"A consistent 40% overestimation isn't random noise there's a systemic cause. I'd diagnose it by examining the data in detail. Diagnosis: Story-level analysis: pull the last 3 sprints' stories. For each one, compare estimated effort to actual effort. Are all stories over-estimated equally, or is it concentrated in certain types of work? Hidden work check: how much time are engineers spending on unplanned work production issues, meetings, on-call, support requests? If 30% of their time goes to unplanned work, then a team with 10-person sprint capacity actually has 7-person capacity. The estimates are accurate, but the available capacity is overestimated. Scope creep within stories: are stories growing after sprint commitment? If a '5-point story' keeps getting new acceptance criteria mid-sprint, the original estimate was fine _- the scope changed. Complexity underestimation: are certain types of work consistently harder than_ expected? Integration work, legacy code changes, and infrastructure tasks are commonly underestimated. Techniques to improve accuracy: Use yesterday's weather: the team's average velocity over the last 3-5 sprints is the best predictor of next sprint's velocity. If they've been delivering 24 points, plan for 24 points, not 40. Decompose before estimating: break stories into tasks of no more than 2 days. Large stories are estimated with false precision. Small tasks are estimated more accurately. Reference class estimation: 'This story is similar to the authentication story we did last sprint, which took 8 points, so this is probably 8 points too.' Historical comparisons beat abstract estimation. Build in an explicit buffer for unplanned work: if 20% of capacity consistently goes to interruptions, plan for 80% capacity, not 100%. Confidence ranges: instead of single-point estimates, use ranges. '3-5 days, most likely 4.' This communicates uncertainty honestly. Rebuilding stakeholder confidence: Short-term: under-promise and over-deliver for the next 2-3 sprints. Use the velocitybased estimates, not the aspirational ones. If the team is delivering 24 points, commit to 22. Transparency: share the velocity data with stakeholders. 'Here's our actual delivery rate. Here's what we're committing to based on that data. This is a realistic commitment, not a hope.' Consistent delivery: hitting commitments consistently, even smaller ones, rebuilds trust faster than hitting one ambitious target and missing the next two. For the follow-up: I wouldn't dismiss the tech lead's claim, but I'd validate it with data. I'd sit down with them and review: 'Let's look at the last 10 stories your team estimated. For each one, where did the extra effort come from?' If the data shows genuine complexity (legacy code, undocumented APIs, shifting requirements from other teams), that's valid we need to adjust estimates for this team's context. If the data shows consistent underdecomposition or failure to account for testing and integration, that's an estimation practice issue. Either way, the answer is in the data, not the assertion."

<summary><strong>TPM-A026 · Your programme has a hard, non-negotiable deadline a regulatory go-live, a contractual commitment, or a market event. But requirements keep changing as stakeholders refine what they need.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. How do you create a delivery plan that accommodates change while protecting the deadline?
2. What mechanisms do you use to manage scope and ensure the team is always working on the highest-value items?
3. How do you communicate the impact of requirement changes to stakeholders who expect everything to be delivered?

**Follow-up**

Two weeks before the deadline, a new regulatory requirement emerges that must be included. How do you handle this without compromising the go-live date?

**What this tests**

Tests the TPM's ability to manage the iron triangle when time is fixed, scope must flex. A mature TPM makes this trade-off explicit and managed.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they understand that fixed time + changing scope = must flex something (scope, quality, or resources)? Do they mention MoSCoW prioritisation, MVP definition, or phased delivery? Do they communicate scope trade-offs proactively and transparently? TPM signal: Do they create mechanisms for controlled change rather than either blocking all changes or accepting all changes? TPM signal: Do they define a minimum viable scope for the deadline and treat everything else as "nice to have"? TPM signal: Do they handle the follow-up by immediately assessing what can be removed to make room? Red flag: Accepts all changes without impact assessment. Red flag: "The deadline is what it is, we'll just work harder."

**Example strong answer**

"When the deadline is fixed, scope must be the variable. The biggest mistake I see is treating both the deadline and the full scope as non-negotiable that's a recipe for burnout, quality shortcuts, and a failed launch. Creating a flexible delivery plan: MoSCoW prioritisation from day one: work with stakeholders to categorise every requirement as Must have (launch fails without it), Should have (important but launch can proceed without it), Could have (nice to have), or Won't have this time. Only the Musts are committed to the deadline. Phased delivery: break the programme into increments. Phase 1 (deadline) includes only Must requirements. Phase 2 (post-launch) includes Should and Could requirements. This gives stakeholders visibility that their requirements aren't being dropped they're being sequenced. Rolling wave planning: plan the next 4 weeks in detail, the following 4 weeks at milestone level, beyond that at theme level. As requirements change, the detailed plan adapts without disrupting the overall structure. Managing scope: Change impact process: any new requirement or change gets an impact assessment before acceptance. 'This change adds 2 weeks of work. To include it by the deadline, we need to remove requirement X or Y. Which do you prefer?' Scope dashboard: visible to all stakeholders, showing Must/Should/Could status. When a new Must is added, something visible moves from Must to Should. This makes the trade-off tangible. Weekly scope review: quick sync with the product owner to review: what's changed, what's the impact, what trade-offs are we making? Communicating impact: I never say 'we can't do that.' I say 'we can do that. Here's what it costs.' The cost might be removing another requirement, extending the timeline for a Phase 2 item, or adding resources. The stakeholder makes the trade-off decision with full information. I maintain a programme burn-up chart showing scope vs. capacity vs. time. When scope increases, the chart visually shows the gap. It's a powerful communication tool. For the follow-up a new regulatory requirement two weeks before deadline: 1. _Immediate impact assessment: how much effort? Can we implement a minimal_ compliant version (not perfect, but legally compliant) in the time available? 2. _What can we remove? Go to the MoSCoW list and identify the lowest-priority Must_ items. If we remove feature X, do we have capacity for the regulatory requirement? 3. _Can we add capacity? Bring in engineers from other teams for two weeks? Engage a_ contractor? 4. _Can we reduce the scope of the regulatory requirement? Work with the compliance_ _team: what's the minimum we need to be legally compliant at launch, and what can be_ enhanced post-launch? 5. _Communicate immediately to the steering committee: 'New regulatory requirement_ identified. Here are the options for incorporating it by the deadline: Option A (remove feature X), Option B (minimal compliance version now, full version in Phase 2), Option _C (add 2 contractors for 2 weeks). I recommend Option B.'_ What I wouldn't do is quietly absorb it and hope the team can work weekends to deliver everything. That path leads to burnout, bugs, and a worse outcome for everyone."

<summary><strong>TPM-A027 · Individual teams in your programme are working well using agile practices, but the programme as a whole is struggling misaligned priorities, integration failures, and poor cross-team communication.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. What are the common failure modes when scaling agile across multiple teams, and which ones would you look for first?
2. What coordination mechanisms would you introduce to align teams at the programme level without undermining their autonomy?
3. How would you measure whether the programme-level coordination is actually working?

**Follow-up**

The teams resist programme-level ceremonies, arguing they already have enough meetings. How do you make the case that some cross-team alignment is worth the investment?

**What this tests**

Tests the TPM's understanding of scaled agile challenges and their ability to add just enough coordination without bureaucracy.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they identify common failure modes: no shared priorities, no integration cadence, no cross-team dependency management, no shared definition of done? Do they propose lightweight coordination rather than heavy frameworks? Do they measure outcomes (integration success, dependency resolution time) not process compliance? TPM signal: Do they resist importing a heavy framework (SAFe, etc.) wholesale and instead take what's needed? TPM signal: Do they respect team autonomy while adding programme-level alignment? TPM signal: Do they address the follow-up by showing the cost of not coordinating rather than imposing ceremonies? Red flag: Immediately proposes SAFe or another heavy framework without diagnosing the specific problem. Red flag: Wants to standardise everything across teams.

**Example strong answer**

"The symptoms misaligned priorities, integration failures, poor communication point to a coordination gap, not an agile gap. The teams are agile; the programme isn't. Here's how I'd approach it. Common failure modes I'd look for: 1. _No shared prioritisation: each team optimises locally. Team A builds what's most_ important to Team A, which may not be what the programme needs most. Result: features are delivered out of sequence, and integration points are missed. 2. _No integration cadence: teams integrate at the end of a phase, not continuously._ _Result: integration failures pile up and cause last-minute crises._ 3. _Invisible dependencies: Team B needs something from Team A, but it's not tracked or_ committed. Team A doesn't know and doesn't prioritise it. Result: Team B is blocked. 4. _Divergent practices: teams use different tools, different definitions of done, different_ branching strategies. Result: integration is painful because everything is slightly incompatible. I'd look at dependency management and integration cadence first because these cause the most acute programme pain. Coordination mechanisms I'd introduce: Programme backlog alignment (bi-weekly, 1 hour): product owners from all teams align on the highest-priority items for the next two weeks. Ensures teams are working on the right things in the right sequence. Cross-team dependency board: every cross-team dependency is logged with: what team needs it, what team provides it, when it's needed, current status. Reviewed weekly. Integration cadence: daily automated integration builds and tests. All teams' code is integrated and tested together every day, not once a month. This catches integration issues within 24 hours. Scrum of Scrums or programme sync (weekly, 30 min): one representative from each team. Three questions: what did your team complete that affects other teams? What's coming up that affects other teams? What's blocking cross-team progress? I'd explicitly not introduce: Shared sprint planning across all teams that kills autonomy. A programme-level standup with all engineers that's a 30-person meeting that wastes everyone's time. Mandated identical processes across teams each team runs their own way internally. Measuring effectiveness: Cross-team dependency resolution time: how long from when a dependency is identified to when it's delivered? Should be trending down. Integration test pass rate: trending up means teams are integrating more successfully. Sprint disruption rate: how often are teams blocked by cross-team issues? Should be trending down. Delivery against programme milestones: are we hitting programme-level dates, not just team-level sprint commitments? For the follow-up: I wouldn't argue about meetings. I'd show the cost of not coordinating. 'Last month, Team B was blocked for 5 days waiting for Team A's API. Team C discovered an integration bug that cost 3 days to fix because it wasn't caught earlier. That's 8 engineering days lost which is more than the 2 hours per week the programme sync would cost.' When the cost of coordination is lower than the cost of not coordinating, the case makes itself."

<summary><strong>TPM-A028 · A group of experienced engineers in your programme says that agile practices standups, sprint planning, retrospectives are slowing them down and adding no value. They want to just "write code and ship."</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. How would you assess whether their criticism has merit or whether the practices are being poorly implemented?
2. What would you change about the way agile is practiced to address their concerns?
3. How do you maintain the benefits of agile - visibility, predictability, collaboration - while reducing the overhead?

**Follow-up**

After adjusting the practices, the engineers are happier but product stakeholders complain they've lost visibility into what's being worked on. How do you solve both problems?

**What this tests**

Tests the TPM's ability to listen to legitimate criticism and adapt, rather than defending process for its own sake. The follow-up tests balancing competing needs.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they listen to the criticism rather than dismissing it? Do they assess whether the practices are adding value or are just ritual? Do they adapt the practices to the context rather than defending the textbook? TPM signal: Do they understand that agile is a means to an end (visibility, predictability, improvement), not an end in itself? TPM signal: Do they engage the engineers in redesigning the process rather than imposing changes? TPM signal: Do they solve the follow-up by finding a solution that serves both engineers and stakeholders? Red flag: Dismisses the engineers' concerns "this is how agile works." Red flag: Drops all practices and provides no alternative mechanism for visibility and coordination.

**Example strong answer**

"When experienced engineers say agile isn't adding value, I take that seriously. They're not anti-process; they're anti-waste. The question is whether the problem is agile itself or how it's being practiced. Assessment: I'd observe the ceremonies first-hand. Are standups actually 15 minutes, or are they 45-minute status reports? Is sprint planning focused on commitment, or is it a 3-hour requirements rehash? Are retrospectives driving real changes, or are they the same complaints every two weeks with no action? I'd talk to the engineers 1:1. 'What specifically feels like waste? Which activities are valuable and which aren't?' Usually they can articulate exactly what's wrong. I'd look at the data: how much time per sprint is spent in ceremonies? If it's 20% of the sprint, that's too much. If it's 5%, the problem might be quality of meetings, not quantity. Common problems I'd fix: Standups turned into status meetings: refocus on blockers only. 'What's in your way?' If nothing, no need to speak. 10 minutes, max. Over-long planning: if the backlog is well-refined before planning, the ceremony should be 30-60 minutes, not half a day. The fix is better refinement, not longer planning. Retros with no outcomes: limit to one actionable improvement per retro. Track it. If the team sees retro actions being implemented, they'll value the ceremony. If actions go into a black hole, they're right to call it waste. Unnecessary estimation: if the team is experienced and stories are well-defined, heavy estimation sessions may not be needed. Try #NoEstimates or simple t-shirt sizing. What I'd change: Reduce ceremony time to the minimum that achieves the purpose. Purpose of standup: coordination. Purpose of planning: commitment. Purpose of retro: improvement. If you can achieve the purpose in less time, do it in less time. Give the team ownership of their process: 'These are the outcomes we need from ceremonies coordination, commitment, improvement. How do you want to achieve them?' Experienced engineers will often design a more efficient process than the textbook. Maintaining visibility and predictability: Async updates: instead of standup meetings, teams post a quick async update in Slack or the project board. <summary><strong>TPM-A030 · Tell me about a recent conflict you encountered in a programme between teams, between individuals, or between stakeholders with competing priorities.</strong></summary>

**Interview prompts**

1. What was the nature of the conflict, and what were the underlying causes?
2. What was your approach to resolving it, and what specific steps did you take?
3. What was the outcome, and how did you ensure the resolution was durable rather than temporary?

**Follow-up**

Looking back, was there a point where you could have prevented the conflict from escalating in the first place? What would you have done differently?

**What this tests**

Tests conflict resolution skills essential for a TPM who coordinates across teams with competing interests. The follow-up tests whether they think about conflict prevention, not just resolution.

**Evaluator guide**

Do they describe a specific, real conflict with genuine complexity? Do they show a structured approach: understand both sides, find common ground, propose a resolution? Do they address the underlying cause, not just the symptoms? TPM signal: Do they stay neutral rather than taking sides? TPM signal: Do they look for root causes often misalignment on priorities, unclear ownership, or resource contention? TPM signal: Do they create structures that prevent recurrence, not just one-off resolutions? Red flag: Took one side without understanding the other. Red flag: Resolution was just "told them to stop arguing." Red flag: No awareness of how to prevent the conflict. Red flag: Provides an example of healthy disagreement in a discussion as a conflict.

**Example strong answer**

"I had a significant conflict between two teams in a platform migration programme. Team A (platform team) was building shared infrastructure that Team B (product team) needed for a customer-facing feature. Team A prioritised architectural quality and wanted to build a robust, extensible platform. Team B had a customer delivery deadline and needed a minimum viable version yesterday. The conflict escalated in a programme sync where Team B's lead publicly accused Team A of gold-plating the platform while customers waited. Team A's lead responded that Team B was asking them to deliver throwaway work that would need to be rebuilt. Both had valid points. The meeting ended poorly. My approach: Step 1 Separate conversations: I met each team lead individually within 24 hours. I listened first, then asked questions. I wanted to understand what each team actually needed, not just what they were arguing about. Team A needed: confidence that the platform wouldn't need to be rebuilt in 6 months. They'd been burned before by 'quick and dirty' requests that became permanent. Team B needed: a working API within 3 weeks to meet a customer commitment. They didn't care about extensibility they cared about meeting the deadline. Step 2 Find the common ground: both teams wanted the programme to succeed. Both had legitimate constraints. The conflict was about sequencing, not fundamentals. They agreed on the end state a robust platform. They disagreed on the path. Step 3 Propose a resolution: I facilitated a joint working session with both leads. I proposed: Phase 1 (3 weeks): Team A delivers a minimal API that meets Team B's immediate need. Explicitly labelled as v1 with known limitations documented. Phase 2 (next quarter): Team A evolves the API to the full platform standard. Team B commits to migrating to v2 when it's ready. A joint design session upfront to ensure v1 is architecturally compatible with the v2 vision, so it's evolution, not throwaway. Both leads accepted because both got what they actually needed. Team B got their API in time. Team A got assurance that v1 wouldn't become permanent and that the architecture was sound. Making the resolution durable: I documented the agreement: Phase 1 scope, Phase 2 commitment, design principles. Both leads signed off. I added Phase 2 to the programme roadmap with a committed slot, so Team A had visible commitment that the platform would be done properly. I set up a monthly platform-product alignment meeting to catch similar tensions early rather than letting them escalate. _For the follow-up: I could have prevented this by establishing the programme's prioritisation framework earlier. When I set up the programme, I should have defined: 'When platform quality and customer delivery deadlines conflict, here's how we make the trade-off.' Without that framework, each team applied their own priority, and conflict was_ inevitable. I now establish a shared prioritisation model in the first two weeks of every _programme."_
